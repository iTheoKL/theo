MedBridge: holding the system together

2026.07

Six contributors, a deadline, and the work of making separate parts behave as one system.

MedBridge began with prescription intake, medication-interaction checks, and symptom cross-referencing. The difficult part was not naming the components. It was deciding precisely what each one could expect from the others.

Six people can work in parallel. Their assumptions cannot remain independent. I concentrated on the boundaries: shared interfaces, session state, and the path from an uploaded prescription to a useful result.

The work was concrete. OCR weights were loaded ahead of use to reduce cold-start delay. Symptoms were connected to the medication data. Session guards and interface changes brought the separate pieces into the same application.

Integration exposed what isolated demonstrations concealed. A component that worked alone could still fail the system through an unexpected format, a missing check, or a delay in the wrong place.

The lesson was not that coordination becomes unnecessary when one person writes enough code. It was that someone has to keep the whole system in view. Under a deadline, an unowned boundary does not stay harmless for long.

To Fall

2026.08 / VERSE

On Icarus, on Daedalus, and on what it costs to make something worthy of the name.

What is a fall

But the memory of a height

Once touched?

Who is Daedalus,

But a name remembered

Because Icarus fell?

Who are you but an artist,

If you abandon your art

When the art demands everything of you?

For I do not seek death.

I seek a life

With an art so great that

It demands everything of me.

And if I fall,

Let the earth remember

How high I once was.

First principles

2025.11

A result is more convincing when its assumptions are visible.

I am interested in software whose behaviour can be explained: where the cost comes from, which conditions must hold, and what happens when they do not. Speed without that account is an observation, not yet an understanding.

The standard is straightforward. Know what you have built. Know where it stops being correct. Do not mistake an impressive demonstration for an answer to either question.
