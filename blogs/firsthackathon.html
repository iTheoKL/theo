MedBridge

On architecture, on the six of us, and on what a plan is worth once the deadline arrives.

A team assembled across two colleges: Pacman and Dino from mine, and from the other, Mr. V, an old friend from school, along with two others I'll call person-1 and person-2. Six of us, all beginners, in the truest sense the word allows.

I had drawn a plan before the hackathon began, the way I try to draw any plan. Not what to build first, but what each piece should answer for and nothing more. OCR to Mr. V. The interaction engine and the data beneath it, the foundation the rest of the system would stand on, to person-1 and person-2 together. The backend to Pacman and Dino. The frontend, and the harder work of making six efforts resemble one intention, to me.

The plan held for perhaps four hours.

The First Fracture

Once the work began in earnest, a different structure was proposed. Not division by discipline, but by module: six modules for six people, each carried start to finish by one pair of hands. It came from person-1, the one among us who had told everyone he understood the ML side best, so we agreed without much argument. I've thought since about how easily a plan gives way to whoever speaks as though the answer were already settled.

We split the modules. We set everyone up on GitHub and connected to Claude, so that whatever came next would at least come from a shared workflow.

Then the questions began. What a .data file actually held, how it should be traversed, what the API keys were for. From the same person who had proposed the structure that made them necessary. At one point we handed over a Claude conversation containing working code, place-your-url-here and place-your-api-key-here sitting in it as plainly as a door left open. It was run unedited. It failed, predictably, and we were asked why.

Person-2 asked none of it, working through his assigned piece without a word. Mr. V asked something else entirely: not how does this begin, but why does this specific thing behave this way. That distinction is small on paper and enormous in practice. It is the difference between building and waiting to be told what building looks like.

What Arrived Instead of a Feature

As Phase 1 judging approached, we needed something committed, something real enough to stand in front of a judge. When we finally asked person-2 whether the reports module was ready, what arrived was a PDF. Not a page that generates one. The document itself, finished in the narrowest sense: existing, and nothing more.

I wrote report.html and js/report.js myself that evening, barely more than a shell, enough to occupy the space a working module should have held.

The Foundation That Wasn't There

Over dinner, the shape of the problem became clear. It wasn't that one module was behind. It was that the foundation the rest of the application depended on, the interaction database, the logic connecting a symptom to a side effect to a medication, had never fully arrived. Person-1's interaction script and database existed, but not in a form the rest of the system could safely stand on.

So Pacman, having already finished what was asked of him, turned toward what hadn't been, and began building around it: a dataset, entered and re-entered until it held. I kept working on the frontend, except by then "frontend" no longer meant one page. It meant everything: login and logout and account handling, a theme system carried across every page, symptoms.html cross-checking patient input against known side effects, and, memorably, a commit that duplicated the entire page body after its own closing tag, leaving two elements fighting over the same ID. I fixed it locally and made a note to actually commit the fix. Dino built the skeleton for the interactions page; I gave it its remaining shape. Mr. V, meanwhile, had taught himself OCR from nothing: an API with image upload support, a preprocessing and parsing pipeline, model weights preloaded ahead of time so a cold start wouldn't cost us. The one piece of my original design that survived intact, carried by the one person who kept doing the work in front of him without needing to be asked twice.

When the Foundation Cannot Be Trusted, You Build Around It

We slept in the sense that exhaustion demanded it. When we woke, the interaction and data layer still wasn't stable enough to carry a live demonstration, and there was no time left to make it so without risking everything else.

So I changed the architecture once more, not the real one, but the one the judges would see. I fixed five medicines into every page that required data, their details written directly into the pages rather than pulled from any live file, anywhere a failure could reach us. It was not the system we had designed. It was, however, a system that would not fail in front of an audience, and by that hour, nothing else mattered as much as whether it would work when someone was watching.

I also designed the order in which the demonstration would unfold:

Scan, Medications, Reminders, Interactions, Reports, Symptoms, Dashboard.

It mirrors how a person would actually move through the product, which meant the sequence explained itself: no page required a preamble. And it solved a quieter problem. Every page already held the same five medicines. Beginning anywhere but the scan would have made that plain. Beginning there let those five medicines appear to enter the system, rather than having always been there.

The Instrument That Wasn't Real

The OCR pipeline worked without fault on a local machine. It did not survive production. And it was meant to open the demonstration.

So we replaced it with something smaller and more certain. Five photographs, matched to the five medicines already written into the application. When an image arrived, we read its filename rather than its contents: Aspirin.jpeg became the Aspirin already sitting in the system, its hardcoded details displayed back as though newly extracted from the image. A short delay, a second and a half to four, kept the result from appearing instantly, so the interaction still resembled a system doing work rather than a lookup returning what it already knew.

To whoever watched, it read as a pipeline: an image submitted, processed, identified, verified. What actually happened was smaller than that, and known to us entirely in advance. Five images, five outcomes, nothing left to chance, which meant the rest of the application could run on a foundation we understood completely, rather than one we could no longer trust.

Dino carried the demonstration itself: four medicines already present on the account, a fifth introduced through the scan, and the rest of the system following in the order I had designed, without a single wrong step in front of the one audience that mattered. Scan a medicine. Verify it. Manage it. Check what it conflicts with. Generate a report from it. Examine the symptoms it's meant to address. Arrive, finally, at the dashboard that holds it all together.

The application never had to reveal how much of its foundation had been rebuilt in the last twelve hours, or hardcoded outright. It only had to hold the shape we gave it.

That was the version of MedBridge we carried into the final demonstration.
